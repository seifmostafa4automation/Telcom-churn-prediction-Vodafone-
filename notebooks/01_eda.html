Session 1: Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)¶

Project: Telecom Customer Churn Prediction

What is this project about? A telecom company (Vodafone) is losing customers, some people cancel their subscriptions and leave. This is called churn. The goal of this project is to use data to predict which customers are likely to leave, so the company can reach out and try to keep them before it's too late.

What are we doing in this notebook? Before building any prediction model, we need to understand the data. This notebook is all about exploring the dataset, looking at what information we have, cleaning up any problems, and identifying which customer traits are most linked to churn.

By the end of this notebook we can answer:

  1. How many customers churned, and what percentage is that?
  2. Which customer traits are most strongly linked to churn?
  3. Is the data clean, or do we need to fix anything first?

4. Cleaning the Data¶

When we ran df.info() above, we noticed a problem: the TotalCharges column is listed as object (text) instead of a number. That's wrong, total charges should always be a number we can do math with.

Why did this happen? A small number of customers have an empty value in that column. When Python sees even one empty cell in a column, it treats the entire column as text to be safe.

We need to convert TotalCharges to a proper number before we can use it in any calculations or modeling.

In [6]:
# pd.to_numeric converts the column from text to numbers
# errors="coerce" means: if a value can't be converted (like an empty string), replace it with NaN (Not a Number)
df["TotalCharges"] = pd.to_numeric(df["TotalCharges"], errors="coerce")

# Count how many NaN values were created by the conversion
n_missing = df["TotalCharges"].isnull().sum()
print(f"Rows with missing TotalCharges after conversion: {n_missing}")

# Show those rows so we can understand WHY they're missing before deciding what to do
df[df["TotalCharges"].isnull()][["customerID", "tenure", "MonthlyCharges", "TotalCharges", "Churn"]]

5. How Many Customers Actually Churned?¶

The column we are trying to predict is called Churn, it is either "Yes" (the customer left) or "No" (the customer stayed).

Before building any model, we need to ask: is the dataset balanced? Imagine we had 100 customers and only 1 churned. A lazy model could predict "No" for everyone and be 99% accurate, but it would never catch a single churner! That's why balance matters.

We check here whether we have enough churners in the data for a model to actually learn from them.

6. Numerical Features¶

There are three columns that contain actual numbers (not labels or categories):

  • tenure: how many months the customer has been with the company
  • MonthlyCharges: how much they pay per month
  • TotalCharges: how much they've paid in total since joining

What we're looking for: Do churners and non-churners look different on these three numbers? If churners consistently have shorter tenure, for example, that's a pattern our model can use to make predictions.

What the charts tell us:

  • Tenure (how long they've been a customer): Customers who leave are mostly new. The red (churned) bar spikes at the very left (0–10 months). Long-term customers almost never churn. This is likely the single most powerful feature in the dataset.

  • Monthly Charges (how much they pay per month): Churners tend to pay more, their red bars cluster on the right side (higher prices). Customers paying under $30/month rarely churn.

  • Total Charges (how much they've paid overall): Churners have lower totals. That makes complete sense, if they leave early, they haven't had time to accumulate a large total bill.

7. Categorical Features¶

A categorical column is one where values are labels or groups, not numbers. For example, the Contract column contains "Month-to-month", "One year", or "Two year", three categories.

What we're looking for: For each category, what percentage of customers in that group churned? If month-to-month customers churn at 50% but two-year contract customers churn at only 3%, that's a massive signal the model can use.

# Start with the four business-critical categories we expect to matter most
key_categoricals = ["Contract", "PaymentMethod", "InternetService", "PaperlessBilling"]

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(16, 12))
for ax, col in zip(axes.flatten(), key_categoricals):
    # pd.crosstab counts customers in each (category, churn) combination
    # normalize="index" converts raw counts to percentages within each category row (each row adds to 100%)
    ct = pd.crosstab(df[col], df["Churn"], normalize="index") * 100
    ct.plot(kind="bar", stacked=True, ax=ax,
            color=["#2E86AB", "#E63946"])  # blue = stayed, red = churned
    ax.set_title(f"Churn rate by {col}")
    ax.set_ylabel("Percentage of customers")
    ax.set_xlabel(col)
    ax.legend(title="Churn", loc="upper right")
    ax.set_xticklabels(ax.get_xticklabels(), rotation=15)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

What the charts tell us:

  • Contract type: This is the single strongest signal in the whole dataset. Month-to-month customers churn at a very high rate. Two-year contract customers almost never churn. This makes intuitive sense, someone who committed to two years is clearly more loyal, and it's harder to cancel mid-contract.

  • Payment method: Customers who pay by electronic check churn much more than those on automatic bank transfer or credit card. A likely reason: automatic payment customers set it and forget it, making them less likely to actively think about cancelling.

  • Internet service: Fiber optic customers churn more than DSL customers. This seems surprising (fiber is the premium product!), but probably reflects higher prices or customer dissatisfaction with service reliability.

  • Paperless billing: Customers on paperless billing churn more. This is likely because they also tend to be on month-to-month contracts, the two things go hand-in-hand.

This is called stickiness: the more services a customer relies on, the harder it is for them to leave.

Streaming services (TV and Movies) don't show as strong an effect, probably because those are entertainment options available from many other sources.

8. Correlation Between Features¶

Correlation measures how strongly two numbers are linked, on a scale from -1 to +1:

  • +1 — when one goes up, the other always goes up too (perfect positive link).
  • -1 — when one goes up, the other always goes down (perfect negative link).
  • 0 — no relationship at all. Knowing one tells you nothing about the other.

We'll look at how our numerical features relate to each other and to churn. This tells us which numbers are most predictive, and flags any features that are nearly identical to each other (which can cause problems in some models).

In [14]:
# Make a copy so we don't modify the original dataframe
df_corr = df.copy()
# Correlation only works on numbers. Convert Churn ("Yes"/"No") to 1/0.
df_corr["Churn_binary"] = (df_corr["Churn"] == "Yes").astype(int)

# Select only the columns we want to compare
corr_cols = ["tenure", "MonthlyCharges", "TotalCharges", "SeniorCitizen", "Churn_binary"]
# .corr() calculates the correlation between every pair of columns — produces a table of numbers between -1 and +1
corr_matrix = df_corr[corr_cols].corr()

# Draw the table as a colour-coded heatmap — easier to read than raw numbers
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))
sns.heatmap(corr_matrix,
            annot=True,     # show the actual number inside each coloured cell
            fmt=".2f",      # round to 2 decimal places
            cmap="RdBu_r",  # red = negative correlation, blue = positive correlation
            center=0,       # white = no correlation (0)
            square=True, linewidths=1, ax=ax)
ax.set_title("Correlation matrix (numerical features and target)")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

What the heatmap tells us:

  • Tenure vs Churn (-0.35): Negative means longer tenure = less churn. The longer a customer has been with the company, the less likely they are to leave. Makes complete sense.

  • MonthlyCharges vs Churn (+0.19): Positive means higher monthly bill = slightly more churn. Customers paying more are a bit more likely to question whether they're getting value for money.

  • Tenure vs TotalCharges (+0.83): These two are very strongly linked, of course, the longer you stay, the more total you've paid. This means they carry almost the same information. When we build a Logistic Regression model in Session 2, using both could confuse it. Tree-based models (like Random Forest) handle this overlap fine.
